ABSTRACTAs Internet of Things (IoT) deployments scale across sectors, Distributed Denial of Service
(DDoS) attacks have grown into one of the most disruptive security threats they face—capable of disabling
infrastructure without any physical access. Defending against these attacks through centralized machine
learning is problematic: it requires pooling sensitive network traffic from distributed nodes onto a single
server, creating privacy liabilities and a structurally fragile single point of failure. We address both
issues simultaneously through a federated learning framework that trains Support Vector Machine (SVM)
classifiers locally across distributed IoT workers while sharing only aggregated model parameters. A
distinguishing aspect of our design is a hybrid feature selection pipeline that applies Pearson correlation
and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) as independent filters, retaining only the features that satisfy both
criteria. This intersection-based selection shrinks the input space by 63% (46 features reduced to 17) at no
cost to detection quality—achieving an F1-score of 0.9881 and accuracy of 98.84% on 500,000 samples
drawn from the CIC-IoT2023 benchmark. Across federation sizes of 3, 5, and 10 workers, performance
remains essentially flat, confirming that the weighted aggregation scheme is robust to scale. False positives
are held to 0.34%, and 98% of DDoS flows are correctly flagged. Compared with Isolation Forest, our
approach improves F1 by 140%; against Random Forest and Artificial Neural Networks, it remains highly
competitive while demanding far less computation.

I. INTRODUCTION
I
NTELLIGENT connectivity has become a defining fea-
ture of contemporary infrastructure. From hospital wards
and factory floors to municipal traffic grids and consumer
appliances, Internet of Things (IoT) devices now underpin an
enormous range of physical processes. Active deployments
worldwide surpassed 15 billion by 2025, with projections

This scale of deployment has, however, expanded the attack
surface available to adversaries. Among the threats that have
emerged as most consequential, Distributed Denial of Ser-
vice (DDoS) attacks occupy a prominent position—floods
of crafted traffic that exhaust network resources and render
targeted systems unreachable, sometimes within seconds of
initiation.

adequately resolved.

requires all raw network traffic to be forwarded to a sin-

consequences are significant: sensitive operational records
leave the devices that generated them, creating exposure
under GDPR, CCPA, and comparable frameworks. In multi-
stakeholder environments—smart city deployments that knit
together public infrastructure, commercial services, and pri-

The research direction pursued here emerges from three

C. APPLICATION DOMAIN
Our framework is designed with three classes of IoT deploy-
ment in mind, each presenting distinct privacy and opera-
tional constraints.
Smart City Infrastructure:Urban IoT systems weave
together traffic controllers, public safety sensors, environ-
mental monitoring stations, and utility infrastructure—often
owned and operated by different administrative entities.
Collaborative threat detection between city departments is
valuable, but data-sharing agreements across jurisdictions are
slow to establish and legally complex. A federated approach
sidesteps these barriers: each stakeholder improves detection
on their own data without any raw records crossing organiza-
tional boundaries.
Industrial IoT (IIoT):Production plants, oil refineries,
and power stations are densely instrumented with sensors
and programmable controllers. A successful DDoS attack
against this infrastructure carries financial consequences in
the range of $50,000–$250,000 per hour of downtime and,
in the most severe cases, risks compromising physical safety
mechanisms. Federated learning is a natural fit here: each
facility can build and refine detection models using its own
operational data without disclosing proprietary process infor-
mation to external parties.
Healthcare IoT:Medical IoT encompasses a broad range
of life-critical devices: infusion pumps, vital sign monitors,
imaging systems, and networked surgical equipment. The
consequences of a successful DDoS attack on these systems
extend beyond service disruption to direct patient safety
risk—a connection that documented incidents have already
established. Federated training enables hospitals to benefit
from shared detection intelligence while keeping patient-
associated data within each institution, in alignment with
HIPAA and comparable patient privacy regulations.

II. RELATED WORK
A. MACHINE LEARNING FOR DDOS DETECTION
Machine learning methods have produced strong results in
network DDoS detection across a range of architectures
and datasets [7].Support Vector Machines (SVM)[4]
are particularly well-suited to binary classification and
anomaly detection, owing to the maximum-margin sepa-
rating hyperplane they optimize. Azimjonov and Kim [17]
reported 98.3% accuracy using a fine-tuned linear SVM on
NSL-KDD, while Alghushairy et al. [18] achieved 97.8%
on CICIDS2017. Neither study, however, incorporated any
privacy-preserving mechanism or considered federated de-
ployment.
Random Forests[5] offer ensemble robustness and rou-
tinely exceed 99% accuracy on benchmark datasets, but
the computational overhead—training 50 to 200 trees per
run—makes them difficult to deploy on low-power IoT hard-
ware.Artificial Neural Networks[6] achieve high detection
performance yet suffer from a transparency problem: their
decisions cannot be audited or explained, a serious liability

Ambusaidi et al. [13] applied filter-based selection to IDS
and attained 99.3% accuracy using 8 features on KDD99
(a dataset now widely recognized as flawed [22]). Their
method combined Pearson and Chi-Square via union, not
intersection. Wrapper-based approaches such as Recursive
Feature Elimination, and embedded methods like L1 reg-
ularization, improve on filter methods in principle but are
computationally expensive—disqualifying them from pre-
distribution federated pipelines where feature selection must
complete before data is partitioned.
Gap Identified:No prior study has constructed feature
subsets for federated IoT security by intersecting Pear-
son correlation and LDA outputs. Union-based and single-
method selection leave features that pass only one test—a
weaker quality guarantee than requiring both linear relevance
and class discriminability to be satisfied independently.
D. PRIVACY-PRESERVING IOT SECURITY
Regulatory attention to IoT privacy (GDPR Articles 5 and
25; CCPA; the IoT Cybersecurity Improvement Act of 2020)
has spurred research into privacy-preserving detection mech-
anisms.Differential Privacy[14] offers formal guarantees
via controlled noise: the conditionP[M(D 1)∈S]≤e ϵ·
P[M(D 2)∈S]quantifies information leakage. However,
strong privacy budgets (ϵ <1) typically cost 10–15% in
model accuracy—a steep trade-off for security-critical appli-
cations.
Homomorphic Encryption[15] sidesteps the accuracy-
privacy trade-off by enabling computation directly on cipher-
text, but its 100–1000×computational overhead is entirely
impractical on devices with sub-GHz processors and con-
strained power budgets.
Doriguzzi-Corin and Siracusa [23] introduced FLAD,
a federated neural network for adaptive DDoS detection
achieving 96.8% accuracy. Agrawal et al. [24] reviewed
federated IDS research broadly, concluding that federated ap-
proaches offer adequate privacy while remaining competitive
with centralized baselines. Both studies, however, relied on
neural architectures (limiting interpretability) and fell short
of matching centralized performance precisely.
Gap Identified:Every current privacy-preserving IoT se-
curity mechanism carries a significant cost: differential pri-
vacy degrades accuracy, homomorphic encryption is com-
putationally impractical on edge hardware, and federated
IDS systems in the literature consistently underperform their
centralized equivalents. No published approach has achieved
both genuine privacy and full performance parity within the
resource budget of real IoT edge devices.

III. METHODOLOGY
A. NOVELTY JUSTIFICATION
Three original contributions distinguish our approach from
the body of prior work:
Novel Contribution 1: Intersection-Based Hybrid Fea-
ture Selection.We present the first application of a Pearson-
LDA intersection strategy for feature reduction in a federated
IoT security context. A feature is retained only if it indepen-
dently satisfies both a linear correlation criterion (Pearson
|ρ|>0.2) and a class-discriminability criterion (top-20 LDA
rank). This dual-gate design stands in contrast to prior work:
•Ambusaidi et al. [13] selected features that passed either
Pearson or Chi-Square—a union operation
•Azimjonov and Kim [17] applied Recursive Feature
Elimination without any secondary validation
•Our approach enforces both criteria simultaneously,
producing a 17-feature subset (63% reduction from 46)
that loses nothing in detection performance

Dimensional IoT Traffic.The only prior federated SVM
study [21] operated on synthetic data with at most 20 fea-

VI. DISCUSSION
A. PRIVACY-PERFORMANCE TRADE-OFF ANAL YSIS
The central finding of this work is that the privacy-accuracy
trade-off assumed throughout the federated IDS literature
is not an inevitable property of distributed learning—it is a
consequence of specific architectural choices. Our federated
SVM reproduces centralized performance on every measured
metric (F1 = 0.9881) without any raw traffic record crossing
a device boundary. The implications extend well beyond this
particular experiment.
(1) Challenges Prevailing Assumptions:Earlier feder-
ated IDS work [23], [24] consistently reported 2–4% accu-
racy losses versus centralized baselines, embedding the belief
that privacy and accuracy are in tension. Our results show
that tension is a consequence of architectural choices, not an
inherent constraint.
(2) Feature Selection as an Architectural Enabler:The
Pearson∩LDA intersection is not merely a preprocessing
convenience—it is structurally important to the result. Re-
ducing 46 features to 17 while preserving discriminative
quality makes local training faster, reduces per-worker mem-
ory pressure, and limits the opportunity for overfitting on
small local partitions. Each of these effects contributes to
model stability at aggregation time.
(3) Linear Structure Enables Coherent Aggregation:
Averaging linear SVM decision boundaries under Eq. 5 is
geometrically well-defined: the weighted mean of hyperplane
normal vectors produces a valid consensus separator. This
stands in contrast to neural network parameter averaging,
which operates over non-convex loss surfaces and can pro-
duce aggregated models that are worse than any individual
local model. SVM’s linear decision structure sidesteps this
instability entirely.
B. SCALABILITY ANAL YSIS
Consistent performance across 3, 5, and 10 workers confirms
that the architecture scales appropriately. That said, the anal-
ysis relies on IID data partitions—a simplifying assumption
worth examining. Real IoT deployments violate IID in at
least three ways [16].
(a) Geographic Heterogeneity:Attack patterns differ by
location. Urban sensors encounter different threat profiles
than rural industrial controllers.
(b) Temporal Heterogeneity:Attack toolkits evolve.
Workers whose local data spans different time periods may
develop models that reflect divergent threat landscapes.
(c) Device Heterogeneity:Traffic statistics differ sys-
tematically across device classes—sensors, actuators, and
gateways produce quantitatively distinct flow records.
Handling these forms of heterogeneity—through personal-
ized federated learning, clustered worker grouping, or adap-
tive aggregation [10]—is a priority for future work.
C. COMPUTATIONAL OVERHEAD ANAL YSIS
Random Forest (F1 = 0.9995) and ANN (F1 = 0.9982)
narrowly outperform SVM (F1 = 0.9881), but the resource
gap is stark.
Random Forest:Constructing 100 decision trees
per worker across 80,000 samples requires 201 s per
round—approximately 150 times longer than SVM. On a
battery-constrained IoT device, a single RF training cycle
would consume a disproportionate share of available energy,
making periodic model updates unsustainable.
Artificial Neural Networks:ANN training at 41 s per
round is more manageable than RF, but opacity is a struc-
tural liability. When an analyst needs to explain a blocked
connection or a missed alert—as regulatory frameworks for
critical infrastructure (NERC CIP, IEC 62443) increasingly
demand—a neural network provides no inspectable rationale.
The learned representations in hidden layers do not map
meaningfully onto the input features driving the decision.

D. ISOLATION FOREST FAILURE MODE
The Isolation Forest collapse (F1 = 0.4173) warrants careful
analysis. Two factors explain it.
(1) Unsupervised Assumption Violation:Isolation For-
est is designed for datasets where anomalies are rare. With
DDoS traffic at 49%, the algorithm has no meaningful mi-
nority class to isolate; its internal anomaly score becomes
uninformative.
(2) Federated Fragmentation Exacerbates the Prob-
lem:Local IF models on 80,000 samples each define nor-
mality from their own slice of the data. Averaging isolation
scores from models that may have divergent normality defi-
nitions does not produce a coherent global detector.
This failure is instructive: anomaly detection methods
built on the rare-outlier assumption break down when the
phenomenon being detected is not actually rare. In balanced
or near-balanced DDoS datasets, the distinction between
attack and benign traffic must be learned from labeled exam-
ples—supervised methods are not merely preferable in this
setting, they are necessary.
E. DEPLOYMENT CONSIDERATIONS
Several engineering challenges stand between our experi-
mental results and a production deployment.
Asynchronous Training:Our synchronous protocol re-
quires all workers to finish a round before aggregation pro-
ceeds. Asynchronous variants [25] would tolerate stragglers
and intermittent connectivity—both common in IoT environ-
ments.
Byzantine Robustness:Weighted averaging is vulnerable
to workers that upload adversarial parameters. Replacing
simple averaging with robust aggregation schemes [26]-
Krum or coordinate-wise median—would limit the impact of
compromised participants in security-critical deployments.
Differential Privacy Integration:Data localization pro-
vides one layer of privacy protection, but model parameters
can still leak information about local training data [27].
Adding calibrated noise to aggregated parameters before
redistribution would close this gap, though the privacy-
accuracy trade-off requires careful calibration.
Model Update Compression:Gradient quantization and
sparsification [28] could further reduce communication over-
head for large-scale federations with hundreds of workers,
but at the cost of modest approximation error.
F . LIMITATIONS
Our study has five limitations that bound the scope of the
conclusions.
(1) Dataset Scope:CIC-IoT2023 is extensive but gener-
ated in a controlled laboratory setting. Live production traffic
differs from emulated scenarios in ways that could affect
feature distributions and model generalization.
(2) Binary Classification:We detect DDoS presence ver-
sus absence but do not distinguish attack subtypes. Classify-
ing specific attack vectors—UDP flood, SYN flood, HTTP
flood—would support more targeted mitigation.
(3) IID Assumption:Balanced, IID partitions simplify
aggregation but do not reflect real IoT deployments.
(4) Static Feature Selection:Features are selected once
before training begins. An adaptive mechanism that updates
the feature set as attack patterns shift could improve long-
term robustness.
(5) Homogeneous Workers:All workers are assumed
to have equivalent computational capacity. Real fleets
contain devices of widely varying capability, requiring
heterogeneity-aware resource scheduling.
VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
We presented a privacy-preserving federated learning frame-
work for DDoS detection in IoT networks, designed to re-
solve five distinct gaps in the existing literature. Our hy-
brid feature selection method—combining Pearson correla-
tion with Linear Discriminant Analysis through set inter-
section—achieves 63% dimensionality reduction (46 to 17
features) while delivering an F1-score of 0.9881 and 98.84%
accuracy.
A. KEY CONTRIBUTIONS SUMMARY
(1) Performance Parity Without Privacy Trade-offs:Ex-
periments on 500,000 samples from CIC-IoT2023 confirm
that federated SVM matches centralized performance exactly
(F1 = 0.9881, accuracy = 98.84%). Privacy preservation,
when properly architected, imposes no accuracy penalty.
(2) Robust Scalability:Detection quality remains stable
across 3, 5, and 10 federated workers, demonstrating that
the weighted aggregation scheme handles varying federation
sizes without degradation.
(3) Production-Ready Metrics:Precision of 99.65% and
a false positive rate of 0.34% are operationally viable for live
deployments. The 97.98% recall rate successfully captures
48,010 of 49,000 DDoS events in the test set.
(4) Resource Efficiency:Memory consumption of 819
MB per worker in the 5-worker configuration and a training
time of 2.22 s fit within the operational envelope of modern
IoT gateways—a significant advantage over Random Forest
(201 s training) and ANN (opaque decision logic).
(5) Comprehensive Benchmarking:Direct comparison
with Random Forest, ANN, and Isolation Forest in an identi-
cal federated setting establishes new baselines for distributed
IoT security. Supervised methods outperform Isolation Forest
by wide margins on balanced datasets; among supervised

B. FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS
Seven research directions emerge from this work.
(1) Multi-Class Attack Detection:Extending beyond
binary detection to classify specific DDoS variants—UDP
flood, SYN flood, HTTP flood, Slowloris—would yield ac-
tionable threat intelligence. This requires adapting binary
SVM to multi-class settings (one-vs-rest or one-vs-one)
while preserving federated aggregation coherence.
(2) Non-IID Data Handling:Evaluating performance
when local distributions diverge—due to geographic, tempo-
ral, or device-class heterogeneity—and developing personal-
ized or clustered federated learning techniques to maintain
convergence under these conditions.
(3) Adversarial Robustness:Analyzing vulnerability to
Byzantine workers, model poisoning, and membership in-
ference attacks [27], then integrating robust aggregation
schemes [26] and differential privacy defenses into the frame-
work.
(4) Real-Time Streaming Learning:Replacing peri-
odic batch retraining with online federated learning that
updates the global model continuously as new traffic ar-
rives—enabling adaptation to zero-day attack patterns with-
out scheduled downtime.
(5) Cross-Domain Federated Learning:Exploring
whether models trained in one IoT domain (e.g., smart
homes) can be transferred to improve detection in another
(e.g., industrial control) without violating privacy boundaries
between administrative domains.
(6) RBF Kernel Exploration:While the linear kernel
offers computational transparency, a Radial Basis Function
kernel might capture non-linear attack signatures more effec-
tively. Developing principled federated aggregation for RBF-
SVM—where aggregation must account for kernel parame-
ters, not just weight vectors—is an open theoretical question.
(7) Energy-Aware Optimization:Designing training
schedules that account for battery state, energy harvest-
ing availability, and power grid conditions would make the
framework sustainable for perpetually deployed IoT devices
operating on constrained energy budgets.
C. CLOSING REMARKS
The role of IoT networks in healthcare, energy systems,
transportation, and public safety will only deepen in com-
ing years—and with it, the importance of securing them
effectively without compromising the data they handle. The
widely held assumption that privacy and detection per-
formance must trade against each other is not a law of
distributed machine learning; it is an artifact of under-
engineered systems. When feature selection, model choice,
and aggregation strategy are designed with care, both goals
are achievable simultaneously. Future work must harden
these systems against adversarial manipulation, extend them
to heterogeneous non-IID deployments, and adapt them to the
continuously evolving attack toolkits that IoT networks face
in the wild.
